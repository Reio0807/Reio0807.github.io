Research practice / Workshop field notes

Doing better research
with AI agents.

Delegate substantial work. Make your intent legible. Build a review process that lets you understand and trust the result.

Notes: Bohan BaiSpeaker: Yoav TzfatiSPAR workshop · 30 September 2026Expanded 1 October 2026

Expanded from the Otter export of How to make the most of AI agents in your research, Bohan’s written notes, and screenshots from Agent Advice That Generalizes. The workshop date comes from the export’s summary. This is an AI-assisted synthesis, not an official transcript. The supplied transcript ends as the demo begins; later points found only in Otter’s generated summary are labeled separately.

The short version

The scarce resource is not just agent capability. It is your ability to specify useful work, assess the output, and keep your attention on the research.

  1. Get one difficult delegation working before adding concurrency.
  2. Own the specification; let agents write and cross-review implementation plans.
  3. Use review reports, concrete examples, and additional reviewers to focus human judgment.
  4. Retrieve relevant past decisions; do not let stale memory become hidden instructions.

01 / The central question

How far up can you delegate?

Slides The workshop frames delegation as a ladder. Asking for one edit is different from asking an agent to implement an experiment, and different again from handing over a research question.

Transcript · 00:34 Yoav’s goal is to help alignment and safety researchers outside frontier labs gain comparable benefits from agent tooling. His test of delegation is not just whether the agent finishes: the researcher should still trust the results, understand what happened and why, and avoid a codebase that deteriorates as work accumulates.

He describes the next step beyond a single experiment as a session that handles a category of experiments: run them, analyze them, report back, and iterate. The intended gain is more useful research output per unit of human attention, rather than simply more generated code.

The slide poses a challenge: if the entire research question can be delegated, consider whether the project is ambitious enough and how much your involvement changes what gets done. Here, counterfactual contribution means the difference your research makes relative to what would happen without it.

Transcript · 00:34 Yoav says he has not yet seen a full research question successfully delegated. He singles out interpretation as a weakness: agents may reach the wrong conclusions or put too much weight on a particular piece of evidence. His view is that outside researchers should choose questions where human involvement materially changes the outcome, since easily automated work may be taken up by the labs. This is his research-prioritization argument, not a demonstrated forecast.

Synthesis Delegate implementation and exploration while retaining ownership of the question, the standards of evidence, and the interpretation. Automatable work can still be useful; the slide’s challenge is a prompt to reassess ambition, not a rule that makes such work worthless.

02 / Pillar 1

Remove technical friction before adding more agents

Give the agent useful access

Slides The access slide advocates Auto mode, access to working context such as Slack and Google Docs, efficient retrieval from previous sessions, a good interface for starting sessions, and a persistent environment that can launch execution environments.

Transcript · 06:18 The practical target is to stop acting as the agent’s manual interface. Yoav gives examples of repeatedly approving routine commands, downloading Google Docs for the agent, or manually sending many similar Slack messages. For outgoing messages, he explicitly recommends writing or auditing the text rather than sending unchecked generated prose.

Separate the agent’s home base from temporary compute

Transcript · 06:18 For GPU research, Yoav recommends running the coding-agent session on a different machine from the GPUs. The persistent session can then provision execution environments as needed instead of being limited to the compute attached to its own host.

Operational interpretation Keep the brief, code, logs, and result locations accessible from the persistent environment. Give temporary compute a defined budget and lifetime. This makes “let the agent run the experiment” a bounded operation rather than an open-ended allocation of resources.

Notes A retrieval subagent can recover relevant code and feedback from earlier sessions. This shortens specification because you do not need to repeat the project’s history every time.

Safety qualification Useful access does not mean unrestricted access. Match permissions to the task, separate sensitive data and credentials, and retain approval for destructive or consequential actions. The slide’s Auto-mode recommendation is not proof that prompt injection or accidental deletion is prevented.

Concurrency comes last

  • First: become comfortable delegating one difficult task from specification through review.
  • Then: add parallel work when long execution periods create genuine waiting time.
  • Avoid: launching many sessions before you can keep track of the work. The slide names context switching and losing track of the codebase as costs.

Transcript · 06:18 Yoav uses multi-hour unattended tasks to explain when a second session starts to feel natural. He mentions Git worktrees as one way to manage concurrent work, but does not give a setup tutorial in this transcript. The lesson is to learn delegation first, not to copy someone else’s number of parallel sessions.

Practical interpretation Parallelize separable tasks with clear outputs and ownership. More simultaneous activity is not automatically more research progress.

03 / Pillar 2

Make hidden expectations explicit

Slides “Fuzzy tasks are underspecified tasks” is the workshop’s framing. Even a simple request to sort a list may hide an expectation about algorithmic complexity. An agent must either infer those expectations or ask questions.

The slide contrasts models that infer intent with those that follow instructions more literally. Treat that as the presenter’s model-specific observation, not a permanent distinction between product families. The transferable lesson is to make important constraints explicit rather than rely on the model guessing them.

A useful rhythm: specification → execution → review

Use the specification phase to discover what you mean

  • Interview prompting: give a high-level objective and ask the agent to interview you until the important requirements are clear.
  • Voice dumps: a few minutes of spoken context can be a useful starting point; turn it into a concise written brief.
  • Cheap probes: explanations, brainstorming, examples, and prototypes can reveal misunderstandings before they become expensive to fix.

Transcript · 11:55 Yoav describes staying with the session until he is satisfied that the agent understands the task. Questions can come one at a time and can double as brainstorming. He stops the interview when the questions become minor and the important requirements are understood, rather than aiming to eliminate every possible ambiguity.

Notes Stay with one task during specification rather than switching repeatedly between sessions.

A specification is not an implementation plan

The distinction Yoav explicitly makes in the transcript’s 22:56 block.
SpecificationImplementation plan
What should be achieved?Which steps will the agent take to achieve it?
Objective, requirements, preferences, and boundaries.Implementation sequence, decomposition, and execution details.
Yoav wants the human engaged here.He delegates this to the agent and asks another model to review it.

Transcript · 22:56 The criticism of Plan mode is now clear. Yoav says he no longer reads implementation plans or uses Plan mode because he trusts agents to choose the steps once the goal is clear. He still values written plans and cross-model plan review before execution. His advice is not to abandon specification or planning, but to direct human attention toward the desired outcome rather than routine implementation choices.

Boundary Delegating implementation does not mean delegating away scientific judgment. If a “step” changes the research question, measurement, identification assumptions, or an important resource commitment, it deserves explicit human consideration.

Insert checkpoints where mistakes become expensive

Transcript · 28:41 Specification → execution → review is a heuristic, not a rigid three-step procedure. Yoav gives two useful exceptions:

  • Highly iterative work: when many small details need human feedback, most of a session can resemble specification.
  • An expensive experiment: for a run expected to take about 20 GPU hours, specify the task, have the agent implement everything up to launch, review that implementation, then authorize the run and review the resulting analysis.

Checkpoint pattern Agree on the objective → prepare the experiment → review before committing compute → run and analyze → review the conclusions. The checkpoint is tied to the cost and consequence of the next action.

Retrieve context; do not accumulate contradictions

Slides Conflicting or stale context makes it hard for the agent to know which instruction should win. Model-written memories can also generalize a narrow preference too broadly. The workshop favors on-demand retrieval over relying on a growing collection of imperfect summaries.

Transcript · 11:55–17:08 Memory is part of the specification bottleneck. Without reliable recall, the agent either repeats questions or guesses preferences it cannot afford to ask about in every session. Yoav’s preferred sequence is: give the high-level task, retrieve relevant preferences from recent sessions, then interview the human about what remains unclear.

Transcript · 17:08 He also cautions against long CLAUDE.md files that duplicate the repository, and agent-written skills that are maintained without human auditing. These can introduce conflicting instructions. His criticism is directed at unreliable or redundant context, not at retaining useful project knowledge.

Notes Keep relevant project preferences available. Combined with the slide’s warning, this means supplying the right context, not placing every past preference in every session.

A compact context-retrieval brief

Template Ask a retrieval agent to return:

  • The most relevant past decisions, with session or file references.
  • The original scope of each preference and whether it still applies.
  • Existing code or artifacts worth reusing.
  • Conflicts between old instructions and the current request.
  • Only the unresolved questions that materially affect the task.

The original record should remain available. A retrieved summary is not a substitute for checking the current code or instructions.

One supplied slide attributes an observation about removing over 80% of Claude Code’s system prompt without measurable loss to Thariq Shihipar at Anthropic. This note records that attribution without independently verifying the original statement. It is not a recommendation to remove safety protections.

04 / Pillar 2, continued

Review for understanding, not just approval

Slides The review dilemma is real: inspect everything and lose the time advantage, or inspect nothing and sacrifice quality. The workshop suggests a review report ranked by the model’s uncertainty, and explicitly asking for an explanation when you feel lost.

Transcript · 11:55 Review is the other half of communicating intent. During execution, the agent encounters surprises and makes choices that were not fully specified. Reviewing those choices lets the human correct or override them. Plots, interfaces, prompts, and prose are examples where preferences often become clear only after seeing the output.

Ask for the report before you leave

Transcript · 17:08 Yoav asks for a debrief when handing the task over for execution, not only after returning. The intended handoff is a prepared HTML artifact explaining what was done, why, which decisions were made, and what alternatives the human may want to consider. That avoids reconstructing the session from terminal messages.

He does not frame the choice as “read the entire Git diff or read none of it.” Instead, the agent can help prioritize what code and decisions deserve inspection, and in what order. The human still reviews and may iterate.

Notes Ask for enough concrete examples in the debrief to exercise your own judgment. Leave comments directly on the report artifact. If you need to postpone review, compact the session first so the work is easier to resume.

Review qualification Model-reported uncertainty is only one signal. Also inspect high-impact choices, surprising results, and a sample of work the model claims is correct. A confident omission can be more important than an acknowledged uncertainty.

What a useful debrief should contain

Suggested report structure, expanding the workshop’s review advice.
Report componentWhat the human needs to see
Outcome against the briefWhat was completed, what was not, and any change in scope.
Evidence and examplesLinks to outputs, relevant code or data, tests actually run, and representative successes and failures.
Decisions and assumptionsImportant choices, alternatives considered, and assumptions that could change the result.
Review prioritiesUncertain or consequential items, why they matter, and the exact place to inspect.
Reproduction and handoffHow to rerun the work, remaining limitations, and the next decision required from the human.

Before compaction Save the brief, output locations, unresolved issues, and verification evidence to durable files. Compaction can lose detail; it should not be the only record of unfinished review.

05 / Pillar 3

Spend computation where it saves human time

Slides Cross-model plan and code review is a central recommendation. The slide also says review can be worthwhile within a single model family. For complex work, use reviewers with different responsibilities: correctness, security, user experience, and dependencies.

Transcript · 22:56 Yoav describes a tool that lets Claude Code call GPT reviewers autonomously, avoiding manual copying between applications. His workflow uses an authoring model to plan and implement, another model to review the plan, and additional models to check the finished work. The goal is to spend computation catching mistakes before they consume more human time.

  • Review the plan: catch flawed assumptions and missing requirements before implementation.
  • Review the output: ask reviewers to inspect the actual work rather than simply endorse the authoring agent’s summary.
  • Scale effort to difficulty: use more computation when complexity and consequences justify it.

Dynamic workflows for families of experiments

Transcript · 22:56 Yoav describes “dynamic workflows” as a way for the agent to programmatically launch many agents, rather than doing everything itself or delegating individual tasks one at a time. He associates this with a feature he calls “Ultra Code” and says it is especially useful for tasks that involve a range of experiments or repeated iteration.

  • Compare a range of loss functions.
  • Try multiple prompts and compare performance.
  • Iterate on a prompt, rerunning the experiment after changes.
  • Build an evaluation and improve a candidate against it.

These are examples from the workshop, not verified current product instructions. The precise feature names, availability, prices, and model rankings are not established by this transcript.

Choose effort according to whether you are waiting

Transcript · 28:41 When the human is actively discussing the task, Yoav suggests medium effort and quick interactions; background subagents can do supporting work while the conversation continues. Once execution is delegated, he is more willing to allow additional time and computation. The distinction is human attention versus agent runtime, not “always maximize effort.”

Notes Bohan’s shorthand records medium → “ultra” → medium. The transcript supports medium effort during interactive work and heavier execution when useful; it does not prescribe a universal medium-effort setting for every review.

Evaluation-driven iteration: what is in the record?

Otter summary only The generated summary adds a “North Star” benchmark with a frozen ideal metric. The transcript supports iterating against an evaluation, but does not contain that particular benchmark formulation. Retain it as a summary-only idea rather than a confirmed instruction.

Research safeguard Improving against one score is not the same as improving the research result. Define the evaluation and stopping rule, preserve failed runs, and check performance on evidence not used to steer iteration. Otherwise an agent can learn the quirks of the metric while the scientific conclusion remains weak.

Cost lens Compare the cost of the full task: model usage, waiting, human review, and rework. “Tokens are cheap relative to your time” is the slide’s heuristic, not a claim that higher spending always improves the outcome. Multiple reviewers can still share the same blind spots.

06 / Additional notes & qualifications

Protect attention without abandoning oversight

Attention and motivation

Notes
  • Use a quieter interface, such as the demonstrated /focus mode, to reduce intermediate chatter.
  • Do not continuously watch routine execution. Return for agreed checkpoints and the review.
  • Long exchanges can be mentally tiring; avoid switching sessions during specification.
  • When motivation drops, reconnect the task to its theory of change: why this research could matter.
  • Flow and personifying agents were mentioned, but the notes do not capture a full argument about personification.

Security and research integrity

Notes
  • Keep crypto wallets away from the agent’s accessible environment.
  • Consider prompt injection and destructive filesystem actions.
  • Check the service’s data-use settings and training opt-out options.
  • The notes connect debriefs and control protocols with concerns about research sabotage; a specific protocol was not captured.

Qualification Stepping away from execution is appropriate only within a bounded task. Maintain checkpoints for sensitive data access, external actions, meaningful spending, or irreversible changes. A good debrief supports oversight but is not, by itself, a safeguard against deliberate concealment.

What the Otter summary adds about security

Otter summary only The summary connects the wallet warning to supply-chain risk from unverified packages or software. It also mentions robust cloud backups against accidental deletion and provider training opt-outs. These details are not in the supplied transcript, though the broad topics overlap Bohan’s shorthand.

Practical reading Keep sensitive credentials outside the agent’s working environment, make recoverable backups, and check applicable data-use settings. Do not rely on a claimed model safeguard as the sole protection against deletion or disclosure. Backups protect recoverability; they do not prevent exfiltration.

Otter summary only Strategic deception or sabotage in debriefs appears as an open question, not a resolved control protocol. Neither the supplied transcript nor the summary establishes that a debrief plugin can prevent research sabotage.

Separate workflow fatigue from research motivation

Otter summary only The summary distinguishes fatigue from repeatedly correcting mistakes or absorbing too much review information from uncertainty about the purpose of the research itself. It recommends reducing specification-phase context switching and seeking mentorship when motivation or the value of a research direction is in doubt.

A useful diagnostic If the friction is repeated explanation, improve retrieval. If it is an overwhelming review, request a more selective report. If it is uncertainty about why the project matters, a better prompt may not help; revisit the research question and theory of change with a mentor.

The flow reference

Bohan linked A computational theory of the subjective experience of flow. Its abstract proposes that flow relates to the mutual information between desired outcomes and the means of achieving them, and reports five experiments. Applying that account to agent-assisted work is an interpretation, not a result tested in this paper.

Possible application Make your own role coherent: choose the question, define success, then examine what the results mean. Repeatedly checking a running agent is not the same as making progress on that intellectual task.

08 / Reusable practice kit

A next-session checklist

Added templates The following is an application of the workshop, not a transcript of the presenter’s prompts or a claim about Bohan’s existing workflow.

  1. Select one substantial, bounded task.
  2. Retrieve the relevant past decisions and check for stale instructions.
  3. Clarify the question, deliverables, boundaries, and acceptance criteria.
  4. Ask another model to review the implementation plan; identify any scientific choices or resource commitments that require your approval.
  5. Let the agent work within agreed limits instead of monitoring every step.
  6. Request a debrief with evidence, examples, uncertainties, and reproduction steps.
  7. Use another reviewer where warranted, then make the final judgment yourself.
  8. Save specific feedback with its scope and source for future retrieval.
Template 1 · Specify a research task 
Objective: [What question or decision does this task serve?]
Why it matters: [How could the answer change our research?]
Context: [Relevant sources, code, and past decisions.]

First retrieve relevant prior feedback and identify conflicts with
this request. Ask me about ambiguities that materially change the work.

Deliver: [Named artifacts and their intended audience.]
Success criteria: [What would make the result acceptable?]
Constraints: [Data, methods, time, cost, permissions, and exclusions.]
Stop and ask if: [Specific conditions requiring human judgment.]

Once the specification is agreed, write an implementation plan and have
another model review it before execution. Surface any scientific choices
or costly commitments that require my approval.

Prepare the debrief before I return: include evidence, examples,
limitations, and a prioritized review list.
Template 2 · Request an inspectable debrief 
Explain what you did against the original brief.
Link each important claim to an output, source, or test result.
Distinguish tests actually run from checks merely proposed.

Include representative examples and at least one failure or edge case
where applicable. Explain how the examples were selected.

Rank review items by uncertainty AND consequence if wrong.
Include assumptions, unresolved problems, scope changes, and places
where the result depends on judgment. Do not equate confidence with proof.

Give reproduction steps and the decisions you need from me.
Write this so I can understand the work without replaying the chat.
Template 3 · Commission a second review 
Review the original brief and actual artifacts independently.
Do not rely only on the authoring agent's report.

Focus on: [Correctness / assumptions / security / usability / dependencies.]
For each finding, give its location, evidence, impact, and proposed check.
Separate confirmed defects from uncertainties or preferences.
State what you could not verify. Sample apparently successful results too.

Return a prioritized report. Do not modify the work unless asked.
Template 4 · Delegate an experiment family with a pre-run gate 
Question: [The research question this experiment family informs.]
Variants: [The agreed range of conditions, prompts, or methods.]
Evaluation: [Outcomes, comparisons, and evidence needed for interpretation.]
Budget and stopping rule: [Compute, time, cost, and maximum iterations.]

Prepare the implementation and have another model review it.
Stop before launching the expensive runs. Show me the setup, expected
cost, smoke-test results, and unresolved scientific choices.

After approval, run the agreed comparisons and preserve the run records,
including failed or null results. Do not change the evaluation or expand
the search without flagging it.

Prepare an HTML debrief comparing results, uncertainty, limitations,
and plausible alternative interpretations. Distinguish observations
from conclusions and identify what I need to judge.

An example for AI × economics research

Illustration for Bohan Instead of “investigate the economic impact of AI,” delegate a bounded comparison: For a specified set of papers, extract the outcome, unit of analysis, empirical design, uncertainty, and stated limitations into a source-linked table. Flag claims that exceed the underlying evidence.

The agent can assemble and check that evidence. Bohan retains the judgment about which question matters, whether the studies are comparable, and what the findings justify. This is a proposed use case, not an empirical assessment performed here.

A larger delegation could compare an agreed family of LLM-agent prompts or experimental conditions, with common outcomes and a fixed compute budget. The implementation and runs can be delegated; whether the agents represent economically meaningful behavior, and what the results imply for AI safety, remain questions to inspect rather than conclusions to accept from a score alone.
